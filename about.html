Who am I?

I'm Ann Dong, a third-year student at UC Santa Barbara studying Statistics and Data Science.

I came to data science through math, and specifically through mathematical modeling. Building models taught me something I hadn't expected: the model is rarely the hard part. The data is — how it was collected, what it leaves out, and whether it can support the question you're asking at all. What keeps me here is narrower than a general interest in data: I like the point where a messy, uncooperative dataset finally resolves into something you can say out loud, and I like being the person who explains it to someone who wasn't in the room.

I draw and I skate — quiet, absorbing things, which is also how I'd describe a good afternoon with a dataset.

What I study

Three fields that overlap more than they look like they should.

Artificial intelligence and machine learning

Most of my machine learning work has been supervised classification on real, imperfect measurement data. In a biodiversity project I compared support vector machines, AdaBoost, and random forests on morphometric data — body measurements — to distinguish eight lizard species, reaching 99.82% accuracy on species separation. The interesting part wasn't the headline number. It was working out which measurements actually carried the signal, and how much of the remaining error belonged to the data rather than the model.

Data science and statistical modeling

This is where most of my time goes. I work with time series and regression — fitting ARIMA models to atmospheric CO₂ and global temperature records, then regressing on the residuals to catch structure the first model missed. That project reached an R² of 0.999 and a Pearson correlation of 0.961 between CO₂ and temperature. But the number I trust more came out of the sensitivity analysis: how far the forecast moved when I perturbed the parameters.

Connectomics

I proofread neuron reconstructions in electron microscopy connectomes. Segmentation algorithms trace neurons through stacked EM images automatically, and they make two characteristic mistakes: merge errors, where two separate neurons get fused into one, and split errors, where a single neuron is broken into fragments. Correcting them means following a cell's shape slice by slice through the volume and deciding, from the continuity of its processes, whether two pieces are the same neuron. Working in CAVE and Neuroglancer, I proofread eight anatomical regions across two reconstruction versions and helped identify neurons that hadn't been correctly mapped before.

CAVE and Neuroglancer The tools used to view and correct connectome reconstructions — CAVE tracks versioned annotations, Neuroglancer renders the image volume.

Circuit-level analysis Studying how signals move through a group of connected neurons, rather than looking at any single cell on its own.

What I'm working on right now

Connectomics annotation

The reconstructions I proofread feed into circuit-level analysis — questions about how information actually moves through a nervous system. That analysis is only as good as the wiring diagram underneath it, and a single unresolved merge error can invent a connection that doesn't exist. I'm continuing to proofread and to help recover neurons that earlier passes missed, which is slow work with an unusually direct payoff: everything built on top of this data inherits whatever I get wrong.

LLM agent workflows

I built an agent workflow that turns a wind turbine's bill of materials into a full life-cycle carbon footprint report — 14 nodes alternating between code nodes that compute and LLM nodes that extract and write. The platform wouldn't pass data directly between code nodes, so the LLM nodes double as typed data couriers, each one instructed to reserialise its input as JSON without altering a single number. That constraint shaped the whole architecture, and it's also the main risk: a language model sitting in the middle of a numerical pipeline is a place where digits can quietly change.

Where I'm headed

Near term, I'm looking for a summer 2027 internship in data science, analytics, or business analytics. What I want out of it is a problem where the data is messy enough that cleaning it is part of the work, and where somebody downstream actually acts on the answer — the kind of setting where a model's honest uncertainty matters more than its headline score.

Longer term I plan to go to graduate school, most likely in data science. The problems that keep pulling me back are the ones where a real decision has to be made under constraints somebody else set, and where the honest answer is a range rather than a number.

Proofreading neuron reconstructions in an EM connectome

An algorithm traced the neurons in this brain volume first. It made mistakes. My job was to find them, one cell at a time, and correct them so the wiring diagram could be trusted.

Tools CAVE (Connectome Annotation Versioning Engine), Neuroglancer

Timeframe [dates]

Role Proofreader on a shared reconstruction. I completed eight anatomical regions across two versions of the reconstruction, and flagged neurons that had not been mapped.

Electron microscopy (EM) The tissue is sliced extremely thin and imaged one slice at a time. Stacking the images back up gives a 3D volume in which individual neurons are visible, but only as cross-sections — a neuron appears as a small blob on each slice, and you have to decide which blob on the next slice is the same cell.

Segmentation The step where software assigns every pixel in the volume to a particular cell. Where it goes wrong, the resulting shapes are not neurons.

What was the problem?

Automated segmentation of an EM volume is good but not correct. It produces two characteristic failures. A merge error fuses two separate neurons into one object, usually where their membranes touch and the boundary between them is faint. A split error does the opposite: it breaks one neuron into several fragments, typically where a thin process fades out or a slice is damaged.

Neither failure is cosmetic. Every downstream analysis of a connectome asks which cell is connected to which, so a merge invents connections that do not exist and a split hides connections that do. Errors are also unevenly distributed — they cluster in exactly the fine, thin processes that carry most of the interesting connectivity. Nothing in the pipeline flags them, which means the only way to remove them is for a person to look.

Why not automate it The tempting move is to write a heuristic that auto-splits suspicious objects. I deliberately didn't: a wrong automatic edit is worse than a known error, because it looks finished. Proofreading only adds value if each edit is defensible.

What did I build?

Rather than a model, this was a method applied consistently. For each neuron I followed the object through consecutive z-slices in Neuroglancer, watching whether its cross-section behaved the way a real process behaves: continuous in position, changing gradually in size and shape, with membranes that stay closed. Where two processes met without a membrane between them, I treated that as a merge and cut it. Where a process ended abruptly, I looked for its continuation on the adjacent slices and, if the morphology lined up, joined the fragments back together.

Every edit went through CAVE, so the reconstruction stays versioned and each change is attributable and reversible. That mattered more than it sounds: I worked across two versions of the reconstruction, and being able to see what an earlier decision was — and who made it — is what makes a shared proofreading effort coherent rather than a series of overwrites.

The judgement call I made most often was to not edit. When the morphology was genuinely ambiguous across slices — a process vanishing into a damaged region, two candidate continuations equally plausible — I left the object as it was and flagged it, instead of committing a guess. An unresolved object is visible to the next person; a confident wrong merge is not.

Both directions Merges and splits are opposite errors with opposite consequences: one invents connections, the other hides them. A region isn't finished until it has been read for both.

What did I find?

I completed proofreading for eight anatomical regions across two versions of the reconstruction, working through the neurons in each region and resolving merge and split errors as I found them. Alongside that, I helped identify and organise neurons that had not previously been mapped — cells present in the imagery but missing from the reconstruction, which no amount of correcting existing objects would have surfaced.

The substantive finding is about where the errors live. Merges concentrated at points of membrane contact between neighbouring processes, and splits at thin neurites and across imaging artefacts — which means proofreading effort is worth targeting at fine structure rather than spread evenly over a volume. The output of the work is not a result but a floor: circuit-level analyses built on these regions now rest on connectivity that a person has checked, so a surprising connection in the data is more likely to be biology than a segmentation artefact.

Why admit limits Proofreading is a judgement task, and judgement tasks need agreement metrics to be credible. Saying so is more convincing than claiming the regions are clean.

What would I do differently?

I would track my own error statistics from the start — how many merges versus splits per region, how long each region took, which structures forced the most ambiguous calls. I have the corrections but not the tally, and that tally is what would let anyone estimate how much error is likely left.

I would also want a second proofreader to independently redo a sample of my regions, so there is a concordance figure rather than my own confidence. And I would keep a written record of the ambiguous cases and how I resolved them, since consistency across a long stretch of this work is harder to maintain than any single decision.

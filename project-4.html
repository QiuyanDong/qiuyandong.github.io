Forecasting CO₂ and global temperature, and the link between them

Sixty years of atmospheric data, three different models, and one question that turned out to matter more than the forecasts: how far ahead is any of this trustworthy?

Tools MATLAB, SPSS — least-squares regression, ARIMA time series, back-propagation neural network

Timeframe Jan 2024 – Mar 2025

Role [Team — I owned the ARIMA models and the correlation analysis. Confirm before publishing.]

ARIMA A time-series model that predicts the next value from recent values and recent errors. The "I" is for differencing: subtracting each value from the previous one until the series stops trending, because the method needs a stable series to work on.

R² How much of the variation in the data the model accounts for, from 0 to 1. High R² on past data says nothing about accuracy on future data — a distinction this project ran into directly.

What was the problem?

Two linked questions about the climate record. First, how fast is atmospheric CO₂ rising, and where does it land in 2050 and 2100? Second, how strongly is that tied to global mean surface-air temperature — and if it is, what does a doubling of CO₂ imply for warming?

The difficulty is not fitting the data. Both series trend hard upward and almost any curve will fit them well, which makes goodness-of-fit a bad guide to choosing between models. The real problem is that all three questions require extrapolating decades past the end of the record, where the models disagree most and no data exists to settle the disagreement. Picking a model therefore means reasoning about its behaviour out of sample, not its score in sample.

The decision I didn't take The neural network fit the data best — R² of 0.999 — and I still rejected it for long-range forecasting. Beyond about ten years it has to predict from its own predictions, and its 2100 estimates drift to obviously implausible values. Best fit is not best model.

What did I build?

Three models, fitted to both series so they could be compared on the same footing. Piecewise linear and quadratic regression by least squares; ARIMA, with the differencing order, autoregressive order and moving-average order each read off autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation plots rather than guessed — giving ARIMA(3,2,2) for CO₂ and ARIMA(3,1,2) for temperature; and a three-layer back-propagation neural network with hidden-layer size set by the Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem.

The extension I'd point to is using ARIMA on the regression's residuals. A regression captures the trend and throws away the structure in what it misses; refitting that leftover as a time series and combining the two as a multivariate regression recovered some of it, lifting the correlation model's R² from 0.924 to 0.936. Small, but it is the difference between treating noise as noise and treating it as something with its own pattern.

For the CO₂–temperature relationship I ran both Pearson (linear) and Spearman (monotonic) correlation before fitting anything, so that the choice of a linear model was justified by the data rather than by convenience.

Agreement as evidence Two models reaching the same answer by different assumptions is worth more than either model's fit statistic. It is also the only external check available when the thing being predicted has not happened yet.

What did I find?

ARIMA fits the CO₂ record almost exactly (R² = 0.999) and projects 497.97 ppm by 2050 and 770.57 by 2100. The 2050 figure sits well above the 685 ppm-by-later-century trajectory implied by OECD projections, which is itself informative: a model driven only by the historical curve produces a faster rise than scenario-based forecasts, because it has no mechanism for policy or technology change.

For temperature, quadratic regression and ARIMA agree closely — 1.25 °C above the 1951–1980 baseline around 2030, 1.5 °C by 2038, 2 °C by 2052 — and their agreement is worth more than either alone, since they reach it by different routes. The correlation is strong on both measures (Pearson 0.961, Spearman 0.949), and the fitted relationship implies a doubling of atmospheric CO₂ corresponds to roughly 4–4.5 °C of warming.

The finding I care about most is the reliability boundary. Testing the correlation model against the two independent forecasts, the curves track each other closely below about 470 ppm — roughly 2040 — and then separate, reaching about 20% error near 650 ppm. So the model is usable for about two decades and not beyond, which is a far more useful statement than a 2100 number quoted to two decimal places.

Why admit limits A correlation model with R² of 0.936 invites over-reading. Stating where it breaks is what makes the part that works credible.

What would I do differently?

The assumptions are too strong to carry the 2100 numbers. The models assume no major climate disruption, a stable ecosystem, and no significant change in energy technology — reasonable for a two-decade extrapolation, indefensible for an eight-decade one. I'd report 2040 and drop 2100, or present 2100 only as a what-if with its assumptions attached.

Substantively, both series are modelled as functions of time alone. CO₂ does not rise because the year increases; it rises because of emissions. A multivariate model taking emissions, land use and forcing as inputs would be a model of the mechanism rather than of the curve, and it would degrade more gracefully when the curve changes shape.

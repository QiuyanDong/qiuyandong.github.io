Planning three square kilometres of land

Given an undeveloped parcel and seven things you could build on it, which combination is best — when "best" has to mean money, environment and community at the same time?

Tools Python, OpenCV, Analytic Hierarchy Process, genetic algorithm

Timeframe Jul – Aug 2025

Role [Team — I owned the evaluation model and the genetic algorithm. Confirm before publishing.]

Multi-criteria decision A choice where the objectives conflict and there is no single right answer — only trade-offs that depend on whose priorities you encode.

Genetic algorithm A search method borrowed from evolution: generate many candidate plans, score them, keep the best, then mix and mutate those to make the next generation. Useful when the number of possible plans is far too large to check exhaustively.

What was the problem?

A three-square-kilometre undeveloped parcel, seven candidate developments — solar array, agrivoltaic farm, crop farm, grazing ranch, regenerative farm, cross-country skiing facility, agritourism centre — and a decision that has to satisfy business planners and the community at once.

Optimising for profit alone is the obvious trap: it damages the land and the community rejects the plan, which costs the developer both reputation and long-run return. So the objective has to combine economic, environmental and community value. That creates two hard sub-problems. The land is not uniform, so "where" matters as much as "what" — and with seven building types across the parcel the number of possible plans is far beyond enumeration.

AHP Analytic Hierarchy Process: instead of assigning weights directly, you compare objectives two at a time and derive the weights from those comparisons. It comes with a consistency check that catches contradictory judgements.

The decision I didn't take I did not hand the raw pixel grid to the genetic algorithm. Segmenting the map first collapsed the search space to ten zones — the pipeline only became tractable because a cheap step ran before the expensive one.

What did I build?

A three-stage pipeline, coarse to fine. First, segmentation: score the parcel on six indicators — transport access, slope, signal coverage, soil fertility, water supply, vegetation cover — render each as a heat map, threshold each to black and white, clean up speckle with dilation and erosion, then overlay all six and cut along the boundaries. That produced ten zones from 0.21 to 0.84 km², each with a describable character.

Second, evaluation: a weighted sum of economic, environmental and community terms. Economic is a discounted cash-flow over short (five-year) and long (five-to-twenty-year) horizons net of construction, labour, hardware and marketing costs; environmental aggregates impacts on soil, water, biodiversity and air; community covers employment and volunteering opportunity. Weights came from AHP pairwise comparisons, all of which passed the consistency check.

Third, search: a genetic algorithm over zone-to-building assignments, with the evaluation model as the fitness function. Each candidate plan is one assignment; crossover swaps zones between two high-scoring plans, mutation reassigns a zone at random. Because the parameters differ by both building type and zone, the fitness function needed 1,120 values — seven building types × ten zones × sixteen parameters — rather than one set applied everywhere.

Read the margin, not the ranking A 0.2% gap between first and second place is the model telling you it cannot discriminate. The sensitivity analysis is where the real finding is.

What did I find?

The best plan scored 2,833,942 on the fitness function; the runner-up scored 2,827,930. That gap is 0.2%, and it is the most important number in the project — it says the model cannot really distinguish its top candidates. I picked between them on a criterion outside the model: the runner-up fragmented single building types across non-adjacent zones, which is impractical to build. Treating a 0.2% margin as a meaningful ranking would have been the wrong read.

Sensitivity analysis was more decisive. Perturbing every parameter by ±5%, most had little effect on the layout — but the economic parameters (unit labour cost, hardware maintenance, short- and long-term unit benefit, marketing) changed the recommended plan outright. So the plan is mostly a function of the cost assumptions, and those are the figures a real client should be asked to supply. For the algorithm itself, mutation and crossover rates barely mattered; initial population size did.

Re-running with a semiconductor plant built nearby shifted the answer as expected: more local demand and revenue, but competition for labour driving wages up and more traffic pollution — enough to change the optimal mix.

Why admit limits A near-tie at the top and self-collected parameters are the two facts that determine how much weight this output can carry. Better to say them than let a reader find them.

What would I do differently?

The 1,120 parameters were compiled by us from mixed sources and are the weakest link: the sensitivity analysis shows the answer depends on exactly the values we are least able to defend. I'd source the economic parameters from real cost data and drop the others to ranges rather than point estimates, then report a distribution of good plans instead of a single winner.

I'd also replace the manual filtering step. Rejecting fragmented plans by hand at the end means a build-ability requirement exists in the process but not in the model; it belongs in the fitness function as an adjacency penalty. And AHP weights are still one group's judgement in numeric clothing — with real stakeholders, I'd elicit several weight sets and show how the recommendation moves between them.

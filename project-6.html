Building a school timetable with a genetic algorithm

Timetabling looks like a puzzle and behaves like a negotiation. This one started with interviews rather than code, because the constraints had to be written down before anything could be optimised.

Tools Python — genetic algorithm written from scratch; mini-program prototype for the interface

Timeframe Oct 2022 – Mar 2023

Role [Led the project — requirements, model, algorithm. Confirm team size before publishing.]

Hard and soft constraints Hard constraints must hold or the timetable is invalid — one teacher cannot be in two rooms at once. Soft constraints are preferences you want as much of as possible: short walks between rooms, difficult subjects in the morning. Keeping them separate is the whole trick.

What was the problem?

Building a school timetable by hand takes days and produces a schedule that is legal but not good — nobody has time to compare alternatives. The scheduling problem itself is combinatorially large and, in the general case, computationally hard, so the goal was never a provably optimal timetable but a much better one, found quickly enough to iterate on.

The harder part came before the modelling. What counts as a better timetable was not written down anywhere. I interviewed four-plus school administrators to find out, and the answers were a mix of absolute rules and strong preferences that had never been distinguished from each other. Turning that into four hard constraints and eight soft ones was the step that made the rest of the project possible, and the step most likely to be skipped.

The decision I didn't take I did not encode the soft constraints as constraints. Doing so would have made most schedules infeasible and hidden the trade-offs; scoring them in the fitness function instead keeps them visible and negotiable.

What did I build?

A multi-objective model with a custom fitness function scoring three things that administrators actually named: classroom utilisation, the walking distance each person accumulates across a day, and how well session times match subject preferences. Hard constraints are handled separately by conflict-resolution logic rather than by the score, so every candidate the algorithm considers is a valid timetable and the score only measures quality.

The genetic algorithm is written from scratch, with crossover and mutation probabilities that adapt as the population converges — high early to explore, lower later to refine, which avoids both premature convergence and endless random walking. I validated it on a pilot instance of four classes over five daily sessions.

On top of that I built a small prototype interface that displays the generated timetable and lets an administrator change inputs and re-run. That was deliberate: a scheduling model nobody can poke at is a result, not a tool, and the questions administrators asked once they could see a timetable were different from the ones they asked in interviews.

What did I find?

On the pilot instance the algorithm produced schedules with no movement conflicts at all, and improved classroom utilisation and timing-preference scores relative to the starting population. What the pilot showed beyond the numbers is that the soft criteria pull against each other. Packing rooms to raise utilisation moves classes apart and increases walking; respecting subject-time preferences constrains which rooms are free. There is no schedule that maximises all three, so the useful output of the system is not one timetable but a set of them at different points on that trade-off, with the choice left to the person who has to defend it.

Why admit limits Four classes is a demonstration, not a test. A reader who schedules for a living will notice that immediately, so it is better to name it first.

What would I do differently?

The pilot is too small to support claims about the method. Four classes over five sessions is a scale where a careful human does fine; the case for the algorithm only exists at whole-school scale, and I never tested there. I'd run it on a real full timetable and report how runtime and quality scale.

I'd also add a baseline. Without comparing against the school's hand-built schedule and against a simple solver on the same instance, "significantly optimised" is a claim about my own starting population rather than about anything a school currently uses. That comparison is cheap to run and is what would make the result persuasive.
